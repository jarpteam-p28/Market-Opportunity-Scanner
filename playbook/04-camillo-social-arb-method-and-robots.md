# Notes: Chris Camillo — the social arbitrage method in detail + the robot thesis (summary in our words)
Source: an earlier Ice Coffee Hour interview Pablo shared. These are the speaker's opinions; his heavy use of options and leverage is not a model to copy.

## The method, step by step
1. **Discover a change.**
   - Look at what people buy, wear, eat, search for and complain about. His main source is reading thousands of comments on trending TikTok videos every night.
   - Conversation comes BEFORE purchases, so what people talk about leads sales.
2. **Connect the dots to a public company.** Ask who wins and who loses.
   - Example: the DIY slime craze, then Elmer's glue sold out, then Newell Brands.
   - Example: bralettes replacing wired bras, so Aerie wins and Victoria's Secret loses.
3. **Needle-mover test.** Is the change big relative to the company?
   - A small division growing fast at a slow-growing company (Elmer's at Newell) can swing earnings.
   - A hit product at a giant usually can't.
4. **Vet it.** Check Google Trends and web traffic. Visit stores and ask clerks what is selling (the Under Armour ColdGear sell-out). Get every piece of evidence you can and let others try to poke holes in it.
5. **Information-parity check.** How much does Wall Street already know?
   - Read analyst reports, stock forums, news and past earnings calls for any mention of the trend.
   - In the ELF trade he called an analyst who had never heard of the influencer video that set it off.
6. **Exit at "information parity."** That's when the trend shows up on the earnings call, in analyst notes, or in the credit-card data hedge funds buy, which often moves stocks a week or two before earnings. He sells then, win or lose. He trades information, not price.

## Where Wall Street is blind (his view)
Wall Street tends to miss trends that are female-led, youth-led, or happening in the middle of the country rather than the big coastal cities. It also misses "nerd culture" (anime, trading cards, collectibles).

## Examples he gave, as illustrations only
- **Longs that worked:**
  - Newell (slime glue)
  - PF Chang's and Cheesecake Factory (regional buzz)
  - Deckers (UGG)
  - Crocs (earlier)
  - True Religion
  - Under Armour (ColdGear)
  - ELF (a $7 primer went viral)
  - Dutch Bros (customers switching away from Starbucks)
- **Shorts that worked:** Snapple (losing shelf space), Victoria's Secret (bralettes), Starbucks (prices too high)
- **Positions at that time:** short Crocs, Nike and VF Corp (Vans, North Face); long Birkenstock and ELF.

## Big picture
He sees big, fast changes as the biggest opportunities (the dot-com boom, the smartphone, cloud, COVID, AI) and called humanoid robots, "embodied AI," the biggest trade of his life:
- Tesla Optimus, plus the private companies Figure and Apptronik.
- Physical labor is a huge share of global GDP, and companies face labor shortages.
- He expects robots in factories in small batches first.
- Risks: timelines (Elon's history), training takes years, and military uses.

## How the scanner uses this
- **Consumer watch** covers brand pairs that show switching: Dutch Bros vs Starbucks, On/Hoka vs Nike, Birkenstock, Crocs, ELF, Vita Coco, Celsius, Cava, Sweetgreen.
- **Each flagged search spike gets checks:**
  - market cap (smaller means a bigger needle mover)
  - short interest (high short interest means more fuel)
  - how much the price has already moved
  - whether it already appears in news headlines

  These produce an EARLY / WATCH / LATE read.
- **The robotics theme includes Tesla.** We can't buy Figure or Apptronik (private), so the agents should note that.
- **The sentiment agent follows the 6-step checklist above** and must say what evidence is still missing (store checks and analyst coverage are things a human has to do).
