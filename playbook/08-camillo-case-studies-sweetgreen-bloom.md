# Case studies: how Camillo found Sweetgreen (wraps) and Bloom Energy, as templates for the agents
Pieced together from the interviews Pablo shared. These are his trades and reasoning, not recommendations.

## Sweetgreen wraps (consumer social arbitrage)
1. **Change he noticed, first-hand:** at his own restaurant, a chicken Caesar wrap became the best-selling item in 12 years. The chicken Caesar wrap was already a trend people talked about.
2. **Dot connected:** Sweetgreen, a beaten-down chain (stock down ~80–85%, a stale menu), launched four wraps built on that same trend.
   - The menu became portable for the first time.
   - The company rode an EXISTING trend instead of trying to invent one.
3. **Needle mover:** a ~$1B company where one hit item can change how the whole company is seen.
4. **Evidence:**
   - A big influencer launch, and lots of comment-section debate. Controversy (wraps smaller than in the videos) meant attention.
   - He asked staff in Sweetgreen stores: wraps were ~20% of sales within ~10 days.
5. **Fuel:** ~23% short interest.
6. **Sizing:** a small, speculative position, because his research was only partial.
7. **What broke it:** a lettuce-related illness scare hit salad chains, which made the wrap thesis irrelevant. Cava also proved the stronger competitor for mass tastes.
   - **Lesson:** another piece of information can overwhelm yours. Exit or re-assess.

## Bloom Energy (infrastructure, contrarian conviction)
1. **Change:** AI data centers need power faster than the grid can deliver.
   - Musk powered the Colossus data center with portable gas turbines because the grid wasn't ready.
   - Gas-turbine makers have order backlogs stretching years out.
2. **Dot connected:** Bloom makes on-site fuel-cell power that can be installed next to a data center, side-stepping grid delays. It's a "pick and shovel" for the power bottleneck.
3. **Contrarian setup:** "Nobody believed in Bloom," with the same short reports every 6–9 months. Few people did the homework; he, a friend and the AI-focused fund manager Leopold Aschenbrenner did.
4. **Adding on misunderstood drops:** when Bloom fell because of a bearish thesis about Oracle that he judged wrong, he doubled down around $77. Knowing WHY the stock fell, and believing that reason was wrong, was his entry signal.
5. **Result and risk:** it ran several-fold (to ~$300). It was then cut ~45% in days by forced selling (a big fund's margin call plus leveraged Korean investors), not by bad business news. He trimmed some on the way up because of concentration risk and taxes.

## The common logic (what the "social-arb agent" copies)
change in behaviour or a bottleneck → which public company is affected → does it move the needle → independent evidence (talk + search + sales/channel checks) → what Wall Street already knows → enter small, add as evidence builds, add on drops whose reason you know is wrong → exit when the information is public (earnings call, analysts, hype peak) or when other news overwhelms the thesis.
